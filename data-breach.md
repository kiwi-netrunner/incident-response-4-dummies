# Data Breach Incident Response Playbook

# Purpose

The purpose of this playbook is to help you respond if your personal information, online accounts, or other data have been exposed in a data breach. It is designed for everyday users and focuses on practical steps to reduce harm and regain control of your accounts.

## What Is a Data Breach?

A data breach happens when personal information is accessed, leaked, or stolen from a company or service you use. This may include:

- Email addresses and passwords
- Phone numbers
- Home addresses
- Payment details
- Security questions or account recovery information
- Other sensitive or personal information

Breaches can happen even if your own device is secure, because the problem originates with the service provider.

## How You Might Know a Breach Has Affected You

You may notice:

- Emails, messages or notifications from a service saying “your data may have been involved in a data breach”
- Unexpected password reset emails
- Login alerts from unfamiliar locations or devices
- MFA (multi-factor authentication) codes you did not request
- Unknown transactions on financial accounts
- Difficulty logging into an account (password no longer works)

## Incident Classification

### Situation A: You receive a breach notification, but nothing looks wrong yet

**Examples:**

- A company emails you saying they have had a data breach
- Your email appears in a public breach notification service
- No suspicious logins or account changes are visible

**Actions:**

1. Change your password for the affected service immediately.
2. If you reused that password elsewhere, change it on all those accounts too.
3. Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA) where available.
4. Check account activity or login history if the service provides it.
5. Be extra alert for phishing emails and messages.

**Outcome:**

Risk is moderate but manageable if passwords are changed quickly and reused credentials are updated.

### Situation B: You suspect your account has been compromised

**Examples:**

- You cannot log into an account using your correct password
- You receive alerts about logins from unfamiliar locations
- Your account details (email, password, recovery options) have changed
- Friends or family receive messages from your account that you did not send

**Actions:**

1. Attempt account recovery immediately using the provider’s official recovery process.
2. Reset your password to a strong, unique one if you regain access.
3. Sign out of all devices/sessions if the option is available.
4. Update recovery email and phone number if they have been changed.
5. Enable MFA immediately.
6. Check other accounts that use the same or similar password.
7. Inform contacts if your account was used to send messages.

**Outcome:**

This is a high-risk incident. Quick action can prevent further misuse.

### Situation C: Financial or payment information may be affected

**Examples:**

- Unknown transactions on your bank account or card
- Alerts for payments you did not make
- Your payment details may have been stored by a breached service
- Your digital wallet or shopping account shows unauthorised activity

**Actions:**

1. Contact your bank or card provider immediately.
2. Freeze or block your card if possible.
3. Review recent transactions.
4. Remove stored payment methods from compromised services if you still have access.
5. Monitor accounts daily for further suspicious activity.

**Outcome:**

Treat as urgent. Financial fraud can escalate quickly but is often reversible if reported early.

### Situation D: Multiple accounts show signs of compromise (possible credential reuse attack)

**Examples:**

- Several accounts show suspicious login attempts
- You receive many “password reset” emails you did not request
- You discover reused passwords across different services
- One breach seems to have triggered issues on other accounts

**Actions:**

1. Prioritise changing passwords starting with:
   - Email accounts
   - Banking and financial services
   - Social media accounts
   - Other high value, important accounts
2. Use unique passwords for every service.
3. Enable MFA on all accounts where available.
4. Check email forwarding rules or filters (attackers sometimes add hidden forwarding).
5. Review account recovery options and remove anything unfamiliar. 
6. Log out of all devices where possible.

**Outcome:**

This indicates broader exposure of credentials and requires systematic password changes.

## Reporting Procedure

**Depending on the situation, you may need to report the incident to:**

- Your bank or card provider
- The affected service provider (email, social media, etc.)
- Your mobile network provider (if SIM or SMS-related)

**When reporting, include:**

- What service is affected
- Approximate date and time you noticed the issue
- Any unusual activity observed
- Whether you still have access to the account
- Any actions you have already taken

**Example:**

“I received a notification that my email address was involved in a data breach. I have changed my password and enabled MFA, but I am reporting this in case of further activity.”

## What Not to Do

**Do not:**

- Ignore breach notifications, even if nothing seems wrong
- Reuse passwords
- Share verification codes or passwords with anyone
- Assume a familiar email or message is safe without checking independently
- Delay action if financial accounts may be involved

## Key Principle

A data breach does not always mean immediate account takeover, but it does mean your information may now be exposed. The goal is to reduce future risk by quickly securing affected accounts and removing reused passwords.
